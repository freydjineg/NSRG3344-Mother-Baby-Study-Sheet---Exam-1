🏥Lesson 1 — Issues in Maternal-Newborn & Gender HealthSlides 1-11
Patient-Centered, Inclusive Care

This whole course is built around patient-centered care — treating maternal-newborn health as more than just the physical process of pregnancy. That means using gender-inclusive language (not every pregnant person identifies as a woman), practicing trauma-informed care, and recognizing that past discrimination or trauma changes how a patient experiences your care. In plain terms: always ask what a patient wants to be called, and meet them where they are.

Social Determinants of Health (SDOH)

SDOH are the everyday conditions that shape a person's health long before they ever reach a hospital. For maternal-newborn nursing, the big ones are:

  • Housing & neighborhood environment — safe, stable housing supports healthy pregnancy
  • Economic stability — can they afford food, transportation, childcare?
  • Access to health care & quality of care — some counties have zero OB providers or delivering hospitals
  • Access & quality of education
  • Community & social context, including the impact of racism
💬 Why this matters
Obesity is a great example of an SDOH-linked risk: limited access to healthy food and safe places to exercise raises the risk of gestational diabetes, hypertension, miscarriage, stillbirth, and birth defects. As a nurse, you assess for these barriers, not just the patient's biology.

Maternal Mortality & Racial Disparities
🚨 High-yield statistics
  • Black women have 3–4× higher maternal mortality than White women.
  • College-educated Black women have a 60% greater risk of maternal death than White women who didn't finish high school — meaning education/income alone does not explain the gap.
  • Black infants die at 2× the rate of White infants.

The U.S. has one of the highest maternal mortality rates of any high-resource country, disproportionately affecting Black, Indigenous, and Hispanic patients. Leading causes of maternal mortality: hemorrhage (the #1 cause — you'll spend a lot of time on this in intrapartum/postpartum content), hypertension, infection, cardiac conditions, and racial disparities in access/treatment.

Teratogens & Fetal Risk

A teratogen is anything — a drug, infection, or environmental exposure — that can cause a birth defect. The effect depends on timing, dose, and duration. The riskiest window is the first 8 weeks (organogenesis) — often before a patient even knows they're pregnant, which is why preconception counseling matters so much.

Reproductive Cycle: Ovulation & Conception

Think of the whole menstrual cycle as your body's monthly attempt to get pregnant. The ovarian cycle has three phases:

  • Follicular phase (days 1–14): FSH/LH mature a follicle; rising estrogen thickens the uterine lining.
  • Ovulatory phase (~day 14): estrogen peaks → LH surge → ovulation (release of a mature oocyte from the graafian follicle).
  • Luteal phase (days 15–28): estrogen drops, progesterone rises to prep for implantation; the corpus luteum forms and supports early pregnancy hormonally until the placenta takes over (~week 12 if pregnancy occurs). If no pregnancy, the corpus luteum degenerates → menstruation.

Conception = fertilization, when a sperm nucleus enters the oocyte, forming a zygote (46 chromosomes). This happens in the outer third of the fallopian tube — remember this, because scarring here (from infection or surgery) is a major risk factor for ectopic pregnancy later in the course.

Embryonic & Fetal Development
  • Embryonic period: weeks 1–8 — organogenesis (organ formation), the most critical/vulnerable window
  • Fetal period: week 9 to birth — organs mature, body grows
🔬 From fertilized egg to embryo — the early journey
The fertilized egg (zygote) divides repeatedly as it travels down the fallopian tube. By about day 3 it's a solid ball of cells called a morula. By about day 5, it hollows out into a blastocyst — which has two parts: the embryoblast (inner cell mass, becomes the actual embryo) and the trophoblast (outer layer, becomes the placenta and membranes). The blastocyst implants in the uterine lining around day 5-6.
🧬 The three germ layers — what becomes what
Once implanted, the embryoblast organizes into three layers that each become specific body systems:
Ectoderm (outer) → skin, nervous system, sense organs
Mesoderm (middle) → muscle, bone, circulatory/lymphatic system, kidneys
Endoderm (inner) → lining of GI/respiratory tract, liver, thyroid, pancreas

Fetal circulation shunts (bypass the lungs and liver since baby isn't breathing air yet):

  • Ductus venosus — connects umbilical vein to the inferior vena cava, bypassing the liver
  • Foramen ovale — shunts blood right atrium → left atrium, bypassing the lungs
  • Ductus arteriosus — connects pulmonary artery to aorta, bypassing the lungs

All three shunts start closing functionally within the first few days after birth as the newborn starts breathing and oxygen levels rise — though the foramen ovale can take up to about 3 months to close completely (anatomically).

Placenta, Membranes, Amniotic Fluid & Cord

The placenta is fetal (chorion/chorionic villi) + maternal (decidua) tissue, fully functional by 8–10 weeks, and weighs about 600g at term. Functions: metabolic/gas exchange (O2, glucose, amino acids in; CO2/waste out), hormone production, and immune barrier protection.

Placental Hormones
hCG — maintains corpus luteum, the "pregnancy test" hormone.
Progesterone — maintains lining, ↓ contractility (labor starts partly when this drops).
Estrogen — uterine/breast growth.
hPL — regulates fetal glucose, preps breasts for lactation.
🚨 Placental Complications (quick definitions — full detail in Module 2)
Placenta previa: the placenta implants low in the uterus, partially or fully covering the cervix, blocking the baby's exit route.
Placental abruption: the placenta tears away from the uterine wall before the baby is born, cutting off part of its blood/oxygen supply.
Placenta accreta: the placenta attaches too deeply into (or through) the uterine wall and won't detach normally after birth.

The amniotic sac (bag of waters) = amniotic membrane (inner, from embryoblast) + chorionic membrane (outer, from trophoblast). It keeps a sterile intrauterine environment and cushions the fetus.

Polyhydramnios
Excess amniotic fluid — watch for maternal dyspnea (fluid pushing on diaphragm); assess for fetal anomalies (e.g., GI, cardiac, neural tube); ↑ risk of cord prolapse.
Oligohydramnios
Decreased fluid (<500 mL) — less cushioning → fetal distress risk; assess for renal anomalies, growth restriction, musculoskeletal compression.

The umbilical cord connects fetus to placenta: 2 arteries (carry deoxygenated blood away from the fetus) + 1 vein (carries oxygenated blood to the fetus), cushioned by Wharton's jelly. A single-artery ("2-vessel") cord raises suspicion for congenital anomalies. Amniotic fluid should be clear (maybe a little pink-tinged) — green = meconium (baby had an in-utero bowel movement, aspiration risk at birth); foul odor/pus = infection.

Twins: Monozygotic vs. Dizygotic
Monozygotic ("identical") twins
ONE fertilized egg splits into two after conception. Twins may share a placenta (monochorionic) depending on how early the split happens — this sharing is what creates the risk for twin-to-twin transfusion syndrome you'll see in Module 2.
Dizygotic ("fraternal") twins
TWO separate eggs are fertilized by two separate sperm. These twins always have their own separate placentas (dichorionic) — no shared-placenta risk.
Infertility & Assisted Reproductive Technology

Infertility = no conception after 12 months of unprotected intercourse (or 6 months if the woman is >35). A cause is identified in ~80% of couples; 35% involve a male factor, 8% are male factor alone.

🔍 How infertility gets worked up
Common diagnostic steps before moving to treatment: semen analysis (checks sperm count/motility — the male partner's simplest first test), hysterosalpingogram (HSG) (a dye study X-ray that checks whether the fallopian tubes are open), and ovarian reserve testing (blood tests like AMH, along with an antral follicle count on ultrasound, to estimate how many eggs a woman has left).

The Big Idea: Expected vs. Unexpected

Nearly every system in the body changes during pregnancy. Your job is to know what's expected (normal, needs education/reassurance) versus unexpected (a warning sign, needs escalation). The nurse's role throughout is the same pattern: assess → educate → reassure → monitor for red flags.

Reproductive System
  • Uterus: grows from lemon-sized to holding a full-term fetus; becomes more contractile (Braxton-Hicks) near term.
  • Cervix: softens (Goodell's sign), turns bluish (Chadwick's sign) from increased vascularity; mucus plug forms to protect against infection.
  • Breasts: enlarge/tender from estrogen & progesterone, darkening areola, prominent veins; colostrum ("liquid gold") begins production at ~16 weeks — reassure patients this is normal even if nothing "comes out" yet.
  • Vagina/perineum: increased vasculature → leukorrhea (white, non-odorous discharge is normal; green/foul-smelling = infection).
  • Fundal height = quick way to track growth: after ~20 weeks, centimeters measured from pubic symphysis to fundus roughly equals weeks of gestation.
Cardiovascular System
  • Blood volume increases up to 50% → dilutional ("physiologic") anemia; increased WBC count is also an expected pregnancy finding, not necessarily a sign of infection
  • Heart rate rises slightly; BP decreases slightly in 1st/2nd trimester from vasodilation (BP should never be elevated — that's not a normal change)
  • A soft systolic murmur can be a normal auscultation finding in pregnancy, from the increased blood volume/flow across the heart valves
  • Hypercoagulability — protects against hemorrhage at birth, but raises the patient's risk of blood clots
  • Supine hypotensive syndrome — the heavy uterus compresses the inferior vena cava when lying flat → dizziness/↓ cardiac output. Teach patients to lie on their side, not their back.
🩸 Where "normal" dilutional anemia becomes TRUE anemia
Some drop in hemoglobin/hematocrit is expected and doesn't need treatment. But it crosses into true iron-deficiency anemia once hemoglobin drops below 11 g/dL or hematocrit drops below 33% — at that point it needs iron supplementation, not just reassurance.

Preconception Care

Preconception care begins before pregnancy — critical because so much fetal development happens before a patient even knows they're pregnant. It includes: routine physical/pelvic exam, BMI, and a full panel of labs (STI panel, CBC, glucose, rubella titer, HIV, syphilis, urinalysis, Pap smear, and TB skin test), plus risk-factor screening, and education on nutrition, prenatal vitamins, exercise, and self-care.

🦟 Zika counseling — a preconception-specific timing rule
Zika is mosquito-borne (and can also be sexually transmitted) and can cause microcephaly and other serious birth defects — there's no vaccine. If a patient (or her partner) has traveled to an area with active Zika transmission or had symptoms, she's counseled to wait at least 8 weeks (partner: at least 3 months) before trying to conceive.

Patient Teaching Topics
  • Nutrition: ↑protein, iron-rich foods, omega-3s for fetal brain development, 8-10 glasses water. Avoid: raw/undercooked meat & seafood, unpasteurized dairy/juice, soft unpasteurized cheeses, high-mercury fish, excess caffeine, all alcohol.
  • Weight gain depends on pre-pregnancy BMI: normal BMI → 25-35 lbs; underweight → 28-40 lbs; overweight → 15-25 lbs; obese → 11-20 lbs. About 3-5 lbs in the first trimester, then ~1 lb/week after.
  • Exercise: moderate intensity is fine; don't start something brand-new; avoid contact sports, lying flat after 1st trimester, overheating.
  • Sexual intercourse: generally safe unless contraindicated (previa, preterm labor risk).
  • Vaccines: Tdap between 27-36 weeks (passive immunity to newborn against pertussis), influenza and COVID-19 are recommended; live vaccines are avoided during pregnancy.
  • Travel: generally safe to 36 weeks (low-risk); avoid prolonged sitting (clot risk).
Emotional Changes by Trimester
  • First: ambivalence (normal even in a very wanted pregnancy!), mood swings, fear, excitement
  • Second: increased attachment (especially once movement is felt), acceptance, decreased nausea/fatigue — often the "golden window"
  • Third: anxiety about labor/delivery, nesting behaviors, heightened vulnerability

📚 Rubin & Lederman — the named framework behind "maternal tasks"
The maternal tasks above come from a specific nursing framework. Rubin's model describes the core tasks as: accepting the pregnancy, "binding-in" (attaching) to the fetus, ensuring the baby will be socially accepted, and giving of oneself to the demands of motherhood. Lederman's related work adds: how the patient's relationship with her own mother, and how well she's reordering her partner relationship, both predict how smoothly she adapts to the motherhood role.
👨 May's Three Phases of Paternal Adaptation
Partners tend to move through three phases across the pregnancy:
1. Announcement phase — the initial reaction to the news (which can be joy, distress, or ambivalence); the task here is simply accepting the biological fact of the pregnancy.
2. Moratorium phase — the partner mentally sets the pregnancy aside for a while, even as the physical changes become obvious; the task is accepting the pregnancy as a growing reality.
3. Focusing phase — beginning in the third trimester, the partner becomes actively involved and starts planning for the birth; the task is negotiating their role in labor and preparing for parenthood.

Variables affecting adaptation: age (adolescents may lack emotional maturity; older mothers face more medical risk), parity (first-time parents = more anxiety, called nulliparous/primiparous vs. multiparous with more coping experience), social/cultural/sexual orientation considerations, and military deployment of a spouse/partner — which is associated with higher maternal anxiety, depression, and stress, though on-base support programs can help.

📋 Psychosocial Screening Tools
Two validated tools nurses may use to screen for psychosocial risk during pregnancy: ALPHA (Antenatal Psychosocial Health Assessment) and ANRQ (Antenatal Risk Questionnaire) — both assess things like social support, stressors, relationship quality, prior mental health history, and substance use, to flag patients who may need extra support or referral.
Cultural Competence vs. Cultural Humility
Cultural Competence
Understanding, applying, and acknowledging cultural differences in care.
Cultural Humility
A lifelong process of self-evaluation, recognizing power dynamics between nurse and patient, and promoting mutual respect/shared decision-making.
🤍 Respectful Maternity Care (RMC)
A framework emphasizing every patient's right to: dignity, privacy and confidentiality, freedom from harm and mistreatment, informed choice, and continuous support during labor. Think of RMC as the "floor" of acceptable care — not an aspirational extra.

Preparing for birth involves choosing a provider/setting, creating a birth plan, and discussing pain management preferences — always grounded in evidence-based practice (EBP) and addressed with awareness of personal bias.

🏠 Birth Setting Options
Home birth is appropriate only for low-risk pregnancies, with a trained attendant present and a clear plan to transport to a hospital if a complication develops — it is not a safe default for high-risk patients.
👩‍⚕️ Midwifery Credentials
CNM (Certified Nurse-Midwife) — a registered nurse with graduate midwifery education.
CM (Certified Midwife) — midwifery-trained but without a nursing background.
CPM (Certified Professional Midwife) — trained through apprenticeship. Scope of practice for each varies by state.
👶 Adoption Plans
When a patient has an adoption plan in place, she — the birth mother — remains the decision-maker throughout labor and delivery: who is present in the room, when (and whether) the adoptive parents have contact with the newborn, and how much time she spends with the baby herself. The nurse's role is to support her wishes as the priority and avoid assuming what she "should" want.

🔬Lesson 1 & 2 — Antepartal Assessment & TestingSlides 1-16
Purpose & Indications

Antepartal tests monitor fetal well-being, detect congenital anomalies/genetic conditions, evaluate placental function/fetal oxygenation, and guide decisions about delivery timing/mode. Testing may be routine or triggered by specific risk factors:

Maternal Factors
Age ≥35 · pre-existing conditions (HTN, diabetes, thyroid) · history of poor outcomes (miscarriage, stillbirth, preterm birth) · multiple gestation · Rh incompatibility · substance use/smoking
Fetal Factors
Suspected IUGR · decreased fetal movement · abnormal FHR patterns · abnormal screening results · suspected anomalies · abnormal amniotic fluid volume

Also: placental/uterine concerns (previa, abruption, abnormal implantation, uterine anomalies) and other considerations (post-term >41 wks, TORCH exposure, maternal anxiety/request for reassurance).

What Makes a Pregnancy "High-Risk"?

Per NICHD, a high-risk pregnancy is one that threatens the health or life of the parent or fetus. A pregnancy can start high-risk (pre-existing conditions), become high-risk during the pregnancy, or even be high-risk before conception. Early detection and timely intervention are everything here — research consistently shows high-quality nursing care directly improves outcomes.

Cervical Insufficiency

A weak cervix that dilates painlessly, usually causing second-trimester loss. There's no way to test for it in advance — it's often diagnosed only after a loss occurs. Risk factors: prior second-trimester loss, cervical trauma/surgery (including prior DES exposure — a medication once given to pregnant patients decades ago that affected their daughters' cervical development), multiple gestation, family history. Treatment: cerclage (a stitch placed early in pregnancy, removed around 36-37 weeks to allow labor).

Hyperemesis Gravidarum

Severe, persistent vomiting → dehydration, electrolyte imbalance, weight loss. Key distinction: morning sickness = mostly nausea, resolves by ~16-20 weeks, patient can keep some food down. Hyperemesis = mostly vomiting, patient often can't keep anything down, and it doesn't resolve on its own. Risk factors: multiple gestation, molar pregnancy, prior hyperemesis (all linked to high hCG). Management: IV fluids/electrolytes, antiemetics (ondansetron, promethazine, vitamin B6).

Intrahepatic Cholestasis of Pregnancy (ICP)

A liver disorder causing pruritus (itching) especially on palms/soles, worse at night, plus dark urine and pale stools, from elevated serum bile acids. The higher the bile acids, the poorer the fetal outcome (risk of preterm birth, stillbirth). Treatment: ursodeoxycholic acid for the itching (first-line), antihistamines/corticosteroids, or cholestyramine as an alternative option; twice-weekly NSTs.

Multiple Gestation

Risk factors: IVF, advanced maternal age, family history. Monochorionic (shared placenta) twins are at risk for twin-twin transfusion syndrome — unequal blood distribution between twins. Every pregnancy complication risk goes up with multiples. Twins can deliver vaginally if positioned well; triplets+ usually require cesarean.

Gestational Diabetes (GDM)

Universal screening at ~24-28 weeks: 1-hour glucose challenge (drink, check blood sugar 1 hr later) → if elevated, 3-hour OGTT confirms diagnosis if ≥2 values are abnormal. Pathophysiology = insulin resistance from placental hormones → fetal macrosomia (large baby, from fetal hyperinsulinemia). Watch for fundal height measuring large for dates. Management: diet first, insulin if needed (glycemic control is the whole goal — good control = no macrosomia). After birth, the newborn is at risk for hypoglycemia (the baby was making extra insulin in response to mom's high glucose; once the cord is cut, that glucose supply disappears but the extra insulin production continues briefly).

🔢 The actual screening numbers
1-hour glucose challenge: a value of roughly ≥135-140 mg/dL is considered positive, triggering the follow-up test.
3-hour OGTT (100g): diagnosed as GDM if ≥2 of these values are met or exceeded — fasting ≥95, 1-hour ≥180, 2-hour ≥155, 3-hour ≥140 mg/dL.
Target blood glucose once diagnosed: fasting ≤95 mg/dL, 1-hour postprandial <140 mg/dL, 2-hour postprandial <120 mg/dL.

One more distinction worth knowing: pregestational diabetes (Type 1 or 2 diabetes the patient already had before pregnancy) is different from GDM (which develops during pregnancy) — pregestational diabetes carries roughly a 5-fold increased risk of major fetal cardiac/CNS anomalies, because high blood sugar during organogenesis (the first 6-8 weeks) is directly teratogenic. This is exactly why tight glycemic control BEFORE conception matters so much for patients who are already diabetic.

Priority is always maternal stabilization first — fetal status depends on maternal status. ❤️‍🩹Pre-Existing (Pregestational) Medical Conditions in PregnancyCh. 7 — Textbook
💡

This section covers conditions the patient already has going into pregnancy (not conditions caused BY pregnancy, like preeclampsia or GDM). This wasn't covered in the slides/transcript but is in your textbook — it's a real gap otherwise, since cardiovascular disease is actually the #1 cause of pregnancy-related death in the U.S.

Cardiovascular Disease — the actual leading cause of maternal death

Pregnancy's normal cardiac changes (blood volume ↑30-50%, cardiac output up to ↑50% peaking around 28-32 weeks) can push an already-diseased heart past what it can handle — leading to pulmonary edema, heart failure, or worse.

🚨 Risk factors for cardiac-related maternal death
Non-Hispanic Black race (3.4× higher risk — largely due to structural/systemic factors, not biology) · age >40 (30× the risk of a patient <20) · hypertensive disorders · obesity
⚠️ Warning signs of worsening cardiac status
Dyspnea that limits activity · orthopnea · paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea · syncope with exertion · chest pain · arrhythmia · jugular venous distension (JVD) · edema

Management involves prenatal cardiology involvement and fetal surveillance. Vaginal delivery is actually preferred for most cardiac patients — it causes less blood loss and surgical stress than a cesarean. Labor and the immediate postpartum period are the highest-risk times, since cardiac output keeps rising further during labor itself.

Venous Thromboembolism (VTE), Trauma & Sepsis
🩸 VTE (DVT/PE)
Pregnancy's hypercoagulable state raises VTE risk 4-5× compared to non-pregnant patients, with the highest daily risk in the weeks right after delivery. Treated with heparin-based drugs (LMWH/unfractionated heparin) — warfarin is generally avoided in pregnancy because it crosses the placenta and is teratogenic.
🚑 Trauma in pregnancy
The extra blood volume of pregnancy can mask early signs of hemorrhage — up to 1,500 mL of blood loss can occur before vital signs even change. Priority is always maternal stabilization first (fetal status depends on it), with left uterine displacement during any resuscitation. A Kleihauer-Betke test may be drawn after significant abdominal trauma to detect fetal-maternal hemorrhage (fetal blood cells that have crossed into the mother's circulation) — this also helps determine whether additional RhoGAM is needed beyond the standard dose if the patient is Rh-negative.

Sepsis in pregnancy is life-threatening organ dysfunction from a dysregulated response to infection — a major contributor to maternal illness/death. It's often recognized through trends in vital signs (temperature, heart rate, respiratory rate, BP, SpO2) rather than one single abnormal number, so prompt recognition of a worsening trend is critical.

Maternal Obesity

Obesity raises the risk across nearly every pregnancy complication on this list — gestational diabetes, preeclampsia, cesarean birth, VTE, wound complications, and stillbirth. It also requires special equipment/positioning considerations, and nurses should stay mindful of weight bias in how care is delivered.

What Triggers Labor?

We don't fully know why labor starts when it does, but theories include: uterine stretch reaching a threshold (this is also why polyhydramnios/multiples/macrosomia raise preterm labor risk — the uterus is stretched "too early"), estrogen/progesterone shifts, oxytocin release, and fetal factors like cortisol changes and rising prostaglandins from an aging placenta.

Premonitory Signs of Labor
  • Lightening — fetus descends into the pelvis; easier breathing, but more pelvic pressure
  • Braxton-Hicks contractions — irregular, painless tightening
  • Cervical change — softening, effacement, early dilation begins
  • Mucus plug / bloody show — may pass as one plug or gradually
  • Nesting — sudden burst of energy/cleaning behavior

💀 Fetal Skull — Molding, Fontanels & Biparietal Diameter
The bones of the fetal skull aren't fully fused yet — they're connected by soft, flexible sutures with open spaces called fontanels (the "soft spots") at the junctions. This lets the skull bones overlap and compress slightly as it moves through the pelvis — a normal, temporary process called molding, which resolves within a few days after birth. The biparietal diameter (the widest side-to-side measurement of the fetal head) is the key transverse measurement providers track, since it has to fit through the maternal pelvis.

Pushing Technique — Open-Glottis vs. Closed-Glottis
🚫 Closed-glottis pushing (breath-holding)
The patient takes a breath, holds it, and bears down for a prolonged count (traditionally counting to 10). No longer recommended — sustained breath-holding can reduce blood flow/oxygen to the fetus and raise maternal blood pressure.
✅ Open-glottis pushing (spontaneous pushing)
The patient pushes with her natural urge, exhaling or vocalizing as she bears down, following her own body's cues rather than a directed count. This is the currently preferred, evidence-based approach.

You may also hear the term "laboring down" (passive fetal descent) — delaying active pushing for a period after full dilation (common with an epidural in place) to let contractions alone move the fetus down before the patient starts actively bearing down. This can reduce pushing time/fatigue for some patients, though practice varies by provider and facility.

💬 Ferguson reflex
The involuntary urge to push/bear down that's triggered once the fetal presenting part reaches the pelvic floor and stretches the vaginal walls/cervix — this reflex is part of why "laboring down" works: even without active coaching, the body often starts pushing on its own once the fetus has descended far enough.

💬 Nurse's trick for palpating contraction intensity
Press your fingertips into the fundus at the peak of a contraction and compare the firmness to your own face: feels like pressing on your nose = mild, your chin = moderate, your forehead = strong. This is how you assess intensity when the toco can only tell you a contraction is happening — not how strong it is.
📡 Wireless & centralized monitoring
Many facilities now use wireless external monitors for more mobility, and route all patients' tracings to a centralized station (like telemetry) so nurses can watch multiple patients at once. External monitors can still lose signal when a patient moves — reassure patients this doesn't mean something is wrong with the baby, it just needs repositioning over the fetal back.
📋 Scope of practice — FSE placement
Placing a fetal scalp electrode is a skill governed by your state's Nurse Practice Act — some states require additional facility training/sign-off (e.g., a set number of supervised placements with a provider) before a nurse can do it independently. Always check your facility policy and state regulations.

The IUPC often has a small stopcock/port that allows fluid to be added directly for an amnioinfusion (instilling fluid to cushion the cord for recurrent variable decelerations) — the same catheter used for pressure readings can deliver this treatment.

Interpreting the Baseline & Variability
📊 Baseline FHR — measured over a 10-minute window
Normal: 110-160 bpm
Tachycardia: >160 bpm for ≥10 min
Bradycardia: <110 bpm for ≥10 min

Just like your own heart rate isn't a fixed number all day, the fetal baseline responds to its environment. Not every tachycardia or bradycardia is dangerous — some common, usually benign causes: a maternal fever can raise the fetal baseline (the baby is "swimming" in a warmer environment), and excitement/fetal movement can do the same. On the flip side, maternal narcotic administration can cause a low-normal baseline in both mother and fetus. These are monitored, not necessarily treated — but persistent or worsening changes still need full assessment.

Variability reflects the interplay between the sympathetic and parasympathetic nervous system — think of it as a real-time snapshot of how well the fetal brain/heart are being oxygenated. Accelerations (abrupt rise ≥15 bpm for ≥15 sec) are always reassuring.

💬 Four more words that describe FHR changes
These describe HOW a change relates to contractions, layered on top of the deceleration type itself:
Periodic — the change is tied to contractions (e.g., a deceleration that happens WITH a contraction).
Episodic — the change is NOT tied to contractions (happens on its own).
Recurrent — happens with ≥50% of contractions in a 20-minute window.
Intermittent — happens with <50% of contractions in a 20-minute window.
Example: early and late decelerations are always periodic (tied to the contraction); variable decelerations can be either periodic or episodic depending on timing.
Uterine Activity

Tachysystole = more than 5 contractions in 10 minutes averaged over 30 minutes. Most common cause = oxytocin. Can lead to fetal hypoxia and late decelerations — treat by stopping/reducing the oxytocin.

When an IUPC is in place, contraction strength can be totaled as Montevideo Units (MVUs) — the sum of the intensity of all contractions in a 10-minute window. ≥200 MVUs is generally considered an adequate contraction pattern to produce labor progress.

✅ Worked example — what a reassuring strip looks like
Baseline ~120-130 bpm · moderate variability (jagged, not a flat line) · several accelerations ≥15 bpm for ≥15 sec · no decelerations. On the contraction tracing below it: each small box = 10 seconds, so count boxes to get contraction duration (e.g., 7 boxes ≈ 70 seconds), and measure start-to-start for frequency (e.g., every 1-3 minutes = a normal pattern). You can't determine intensity from the graph alone — that still requires palpation or an IUPC reading. Put it together: normal baseline + moderate variability + accelerations + no decels + a reasonable contraction pattern = a reassuring strip, full stop.
Three-Tier FHR Interpretation System
CategoryCriteria
I — NormalBaseline 110-160, moderate variability, no late/variable decels, early decels/accelerations present or absent

III — AbnormalAbsent variability WITH recurrent late decels, recurrent variable decels, OR bradycardia — or a sinusoidal pattern
🚨 Category III = act immediately
Indicates likely fetal acidemia/hypoxia — immediate intervention/possible expedited delivery.
Intrauterine Resuscitation — the standard bundle
🔄 In order
1. Reposition the mother (lateral) — relieves cord compression & supine hypotension
2. IV fluid bolus (≥500 mL LR)
3. Discontinue oxytocin if infusing
4. Administer O2 via nonrebreather (10 L/min) if indicated by fetal response
5. Notify the provider
6. Consider amnioinfusion (variable decels) or terbutaline (reduce contractions/tachysystole)
7. If hypotension persists despite repositioning/fluids (e.g., after an epidural), consider ephedrine 5-10 mg IV push to raise maternal BP — it directly affects fetal perfusion
8. Prepare for expedited delivery if the pattern doesn't resolve

The resuscitation bundle serves four overlapping goals depending on the concerning finding: promote fetal oxygenation (reposition, fluids, O2), reduce uterine activity (stop oxytocin, consider terbutaline), relieve cord compression (reposition, amnioinfusion), and correct maternal hypotension (reposition, fluids, ephedrine). Many of the same first steps address multiple goals at once — which is why repositioning and a fluid bolus come first almost regardless of the specific concerning pattern.

💧 Amnioinfusion — typical volumes
A bolus of 250-500 mL given over 20-30 minutes, or a continuous infusion of 120-180 mL/hr up to 1,000 mL total — room-temperature saline or lactated Ringer's is instilled through the IUPC to cushion the cord.
